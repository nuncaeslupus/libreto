Lena looked at the coin, then at the door behind her, then at nothing at all. “Nobody came in at nine. Nobody’s been in that room since Tuesday.”

The jukebox clicked over to a new record, an old one, all brass and static, and neither of them said anything until it had finished.

“Lena,” she said quietly, “if he’s in trouble, I’d rather hear it from you.”

“Then hear this.” Lena leaned close enough that Vega could smell the lemon on her hands. “Go home. Don’t come back to the Blue Lamp tomorrow.”

3

She did not answer. There was nothing in the room that could help her, only the ring of the coin on the wood and the smell of lemons, and Lena’s hands, which would not be still.

Outside, the rain had turned to the fine kind that does not fall so much as hang. Vega walked the long way around, past the station steps, because it was the way Kit always walked.

The street was empty. A bus went by with its lights on and nobody in it, and the puddles took the light and broke it, and she walked on through them.

She had asked him once, on the station steps, whether he would ever lie to her. Kit said no.

28

Behind her the door of the Blue Lamp closed on its slow spring, and the music thinned to a thread and then to nothing, and for a moment she could hear only the rain on the awning.

He said it too fast, the way people answer a question they have rehearsed on the walk over, and she had let it go because it was late.

At the corner of Harbor Street she stopped under the awning of the pawnshop and counted the windows above the Blue Lamp. Three dark, one lit.
